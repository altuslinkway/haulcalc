Labels used: **[OFFICIAL]** means a government conversion factor. **[INDUSTRY]** means dumpster, trailer, or manufacturer figures. **[OPERATOR]** means a junk-hauler or vendor rule of thumb. **[CALC]** means my own arithmetic.

## 1. Reference volumes: items, vehicles, trailer math, and loose-pile compression

### Takeaway
Operators work in cubic yards (L×W×H in feet ÷ 27). Typical rule-of-thumb volumes: a standard sofa ≈ 2 yd³, sectional 4–6, large dresser ≈ 1.5, queen mattress ≈ 1–1.5, washer or dryer 0.5–1 each, treadmill 1–1.5, piano 3–4, and a cut-up hot tub 5–8. A pickup bed holds about 1.5–3 yd³. A 7x14 dump trailer holds about 7 yd³ with 2-ft sides and about 14 with 4-ft sides, a 7x16 about 8.3 and 16.6, and a junk truck body usually 14–18 yd³. No published, junk-specific compaction factor exists. The only published one, 0.7–0.8×, comes from a general debris calculator and describes broken-down, tightly packed loads. Weight caps often bind before volume does on dense material.

### Inferences
- [CALC] Queen box spring (~60×80×9 in) ≈ 0.9 yd³, so a queen mattress plus box spring ≈ 2–2.5 yd³ as loaded. No source gave a box-spring figure.
- [CALC] Nominal 8×12×4 = 14.2 yd³ and 8×12×5 = 17.8 yd³, so "15/18-yard" bodies are rounded-up labels. Interior width on "7-ft" trailers (80–82 in) cuts nominal volume about 3–5%. An app should compute volume from interior dimensions the operator enters.
- For a photo app: item-level defaults should be ranges with a midpoint, e.g., sofa 2 (1.5–3), sectional 5 (4–6), armchair/recliner 1 (1–1.5), dresser 1.5 (1–1.5), queen mattress 1.25 (1–1.5), washer/dryer 0.75 (0.5–1), fridge 1.5–2, treadmill 1.25, piano 3.5, hot tub 6.5 (cut up), 30-gal bag 0.15 (1/6–1/8 yd³). Loose-pile estimates should probably be multiplied by about 0.8 when the crew can break items down. No junk-specific source supports a fixed number; operators should calibrate with their own estimate-vs-actual data.
- Heaped loads appear to add ~20% over level-full on open-top bodies (one trailer example). Many operators load above the rails on trucks, which partly explains why "pickup loads" are quoted at 2–5 yd³.

### Gaps
- No credible volume figures for: dining set (table + chairs), box spring, recliner specifically, full/king/twin mattresses, stackable laundry units, chest freezers.
- No junk-industry study of loose-pile vs. loaded-volume compression. Reddit r/junkremoval and r/sweatystartup were not searchable, and operator YouTube transcripts were not accessible.
- 1-800-GOT-JUNK's official truck dimensions and fractional-tier definitions could not be read directly (site blocked).

## 2. Weight references: lb per cubic yard by material and single-item weights

### Takeaway
Official factors (EPA's April 2016 memo, which updated 1997 Appendix B from secondary data) put uncompacted residential MSW at ~250–300 lb/yd³, major appliances at 145, mixed yard waste at 250 (640 compacted), lumber at 169, asphalt roofing at 731, and "concrete" at 860 lb/yd³. The concrete figure is far below the industry figures of ~2,000 lb/yd³ for broken concrete and ~4,050 for solid. Industry rules of thumb: household junk 150–400 lb/yd³; drywall debris 500–800; dirt 2,000–3,000; tear-off shingles ~240 lb/square (3-tab) to ~420 (architectural), ≈ 0.5 yd³ per square per layer. Weight caps on trailers (~6,600–9,800 lb payload) and truck tiers bind on dense loads long before volume.

### Cited Findings

**[OFFICIAL] EPA "Volume-to-Weight Conversion Factors," April 2016 (Office of Resource Conservation and Recovery)** — [EPA memo PDF](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2016-04/documents/volume_to_weight_conversion_factors_memorandum_04192016_508fnl.pdf)
- Background: the memo updates the 1997 "Measuring Recycling" Appendix B, expands it from 12 to 15 material categories (carpet, commingled recyclables, electronics, and C&D are new), and relies on secondary data. "Primary data collection was not performed," and some original factors were dropped "due to lack of updated data." It has no furniture or mattress rows.
- Residential MSW: uncompacted 250–300 lb/yd³; compacted 400–700; multifamily uncompacted 95. The row label was cut off in the excerpt, so verify.
- Major appliances: 145 lb/yd³.
- Yard trimmings: leaves 250–500 lb/yd³; mixed yard waste 250 uncompacted / 640 compacted; prunings and trimmings 127; branches and stumps 127.
- Wood: clean dimensional lumber, other recyclable wood, painted/stained wood, and treated wood 169 lb/yd³ each; clean engineered wood 268; wood chips 473 (green) / 243 (dry).
- C&D concrete (large or small, with or without rebar): 860 lb/yd³. This conflicts sharply with industry figures below.
- Asphalt roofing ("composition roofing / other asphalt roofing"): 731 lb/yd³. A Lincoln, NE fact sheet citing the memo labels it "shingles … 731 lbs/cubic yard" — [Lincoln NE estimating material weight](https://www.lincoln.ne.gov/files/sharedassets/public/v/1/ltu/utilities/solid-waste-management/recycling/commercial-fact-sheets/estimating-material-weight.pdf). The older EPA-hosted table lists asphalt/shingles composition, loose, at 418.5 lb/yd³ — [EPA older conversions sheet](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2016-03/documents/conversions.pdf)
- Drywall: no 2016 row was retrievable. A C&D toolkit citing 1997 EPA Appendix B lists drywall at 500 lb/yd³ — [Syracuse Land Bank C&D toolkit](http://syracuselandbank.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/07/CD-weight-to-volume-calculation-Waste-Cap-from-other-sources.pdf). ### Inferences
- The EPA 2016 concrete factor (860 lb/yd³) seems to reflect a mixed or loose recycling-stream measurement and should not be used for a junk app's broken-concrete weight. Operators and Budget Dumpster use ~2,000 lb/yd³ broken and ~4,000 solid. Show both and label them.
- Suggested default densities for a photo-quoting app, as a synthesis (lb per loose yd³): mixed household junk 200–300 (midpoint 250, matching the EPA uncompacted residential MSW factor); furniture-heavy loads ~150–250; appliances ~145 (EPA) or per-item weights; yard waste 250 (leaves 250–500, brush ~130); lumber/wood ~170–270; drywall ~500–600; mixed C&D ~420–500 (Florida DEP measured 484); shingles ~600–750 loose (or 240–420 per square); broken concrete or brick ~2,000–3,000; dirt ~2,000–3,000, or +40–50% if wet.
- [CALC] A 1,600 lb tier cap holds only ≈0.4 yd³ of solid concrete or ≈0.6–0.8 yd³ of dirt. A 7x16 with ~9,000 lb payload holds ~4.5 yd³ of broken concrete at 2,000 lb/yd³. An app needs a separate "dense material" path that prices by weight or bed fraction, not volume.

### Gaps
- Could not open the EPA memo directly to verify each row (blocked). The mixed-C&D, drywall, and brick rows from the 2016 table were not retrieved.
- No credible weight found for a standard land treadmill, dining set, dresser, sectional, or upright piano alone.
- No official per-yd³ factor for furniture or bulky household items. EPA 2016 has no furniture row.

## 4. How photo/virtual quoting tools work, and how accurate they are

### Takeaway
The big brands split. 1-800-GOT-JUNK quotes only on site for multi-item jobs and offers instant online pricing only for single items. LoadUp gives an item-based "guaranteed upfront" online price, conditioned on ZIP and pickup conditions. - Training or calibrating on an operator's own historical photo-to-invoice data appears to be the main lever on accuracy, according to the vendors' own claims.
- Item-based instant pricing (the LoadUp model) avoids volume estimation but needs access inputs and per-item tables. Volume-based photo estimating needs scale references and multiple angles.

## 5. What operators ask customers to include in photos and texts

### Takeaway
The standard ask is two wide shots of each pile from different angles, close-ups of heavy or surcharge items, a photo of the access route and truck parking, and a scale reference such as a door, trash bin, or person. The text should cover address or cross streets, number and location of piles, floor and stairs/elevator, carry distance, HOA or parking and building rules, and anything hazardous or needing special tools. Staging items in one spot improves accuracy.

### Cited Findings
- Wide shot of the whole pile ("the most important photo because it shows volume"); a second wide angle to show the true footprint; close-ups of heavy or special items (mattresses, fridges, AC units, TVs, tile, dirt, concrete); a photo of the access route (stairs, hallways, gates, distance); where the truck can park; a scale reference ("a standard door, a sofa, a trash bin, a person"); staged items get the most accurate estimate; multiple piles should each get wide shots, labeled by location — [Remoovit, Jan 20 2026](https://blog.remoovit.com/2026/01/20/how-to-get-an-accurate-junk-removal-estimate-from-photos/)
- Three-shot protocol: a wide view for volume, a close-up for material density, and an access shot showing stairs and parking. Add a photo of any likely surcharge item (mattress, tire, fridge, TV, hot tub) to reduce "on-site adjustment" risk.
